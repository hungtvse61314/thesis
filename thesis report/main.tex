\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Recruitment screening is a high-volume information filtering problem in which recruiters must identify a small number of relevant candidates from a large collection of curriculum vitae (CVs). Existing applicant tracking systems and similarity-based methods often rely heavily on exact keywords or overall semantic similarity. Consequently, they may rank a CV highly even when the candidate does not satisfy mandatory experience, seniority, responsibility, or evidence requirements. A fresher CV, for example, may mention Java, Spring Boot, SQL, and Docker and therefore appear relevant to a senior Java role despite lacking the required years of experience, production ownership, system design, and mentoring evidence.

This thesis proposes an AI-driven recruitment screening system that formulates English IT CV--JD matching as a ranking problem and introduces a \emph{requirement-aware and evidence-aware matching mechanism with constraint-based reranking}. The system ingests PDF CVs, applies direct text extraction, and falls back to optical character recognition (OCR) for scanned documents. It then parses CV and job description (JD) content into structured sections, decomposes each JD into mandatory and optional requirements, retrieves supporting evidence from Work Experience and Projects, classifies each requirement as satisfied, partially satisfied, unsupported, or not satisfied, and validates hard constraints such as minimum years of experience and seniority level.

The experimental design combines 2,986 real scanned PDF CVs from the public Kaggle \emph{Resume Dataset PDF} \citep{hadikpresumepdf} with 1,000 controlled AI-generated English IT CVs. The real corpus is used for OCR, parsing, and practical ranking evaluation, whereas the synthetic corpus supports controlled seniority-mismatch, unsupported-claim, keyword-stuffing, and scalability experiments. Synthetic data is not used as the sole evidence of matching effectiveness; the primary ranking conclusions are based on a manually judged pool of 246 CV--JD pairs across five representative Senior/Junior IT job descriptions.

The proposed approach is compared with TF--IDF, SBERT full-document similarity, and section-level SBERT baselines. Evaluation uses NDCG@5, NDCG@10, MAP, MRR, Recall@10, Recall@20, hard-constraint gating behavior, keyword-stuffing rank change, OCR reliability, and operational latency, together with bootstrap confidence intervals and a paired significance test. Against the human-labeled pool, the three similarity-only baselines rank ahead of the proposed requirement-aware, evidence-aware model on every ranking metric except MRR; this thesis reports that result directly rather than only the cases where the proposed mechanism wins, and traces it to two causes -- an auto-parsed hard-requirement list that is rarely satisfied in full, and evidence-similarity thresholds that a proper held-out calibration shows were left too lenient by default (calibrating them closes roughly half the gap but not all of it). A battery of controlled robustness tests, each category built as three constructed instances rather than one, shows the mechanism reliably does what it was designed to do for seniority mismatch, missing mandatory requirements, and keyword stuffing, but is fooled by descriptor-language role confusion in the majority of tested instances -- a genuine weakness the single-instance version of the same test did not reveal. The intended outcome is a practical and auditable decision-support prototype that makes requirement coverage and evidence explicit to a reviewer, improving initial CV screening while keeping final hiring decisions under human control; redesigning the JD parser's hard/optional classification and scoping declared-skill extraction to genuine personal-skill statements are identified as the two most concrete next steps rather than resolved contributions of this thesis.

\textbf{Keywords:} recruitment screening, CV--JD matching, semantic information retrieval, requirement decomposition, evidence-aware matching, constraint-based reranking, SBERT, OCR, explainable AI.

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background}
Recruitment screening is one of the earliest and most time-consuming stages in the hiring process. For a single opening, recruiters may receive tens or hundreds of CVs. The challenge is particularly significant in information technology recruitment because technical roles are described using diverse terminology, abbreviations, frameworks, cloud platforms, and project responsibilities. A backend position may require Java, Spring Boot, REST APIs, microservices, SQL, Docker, Kubernetes, and cloud deployment, while candidates may describe similar experience using different phrases such as enterprise services, distributed systems, containerized deployment, or cloud-native development.

Traditional applicant tracking systems generally support keyword search, Boolean rules, and manual filters. These tools are efficient for exact matching but are sensitive to vocabulary mismatch and keyword stuffing. Semantic retrieval models reduce vocabulary mismatch by representing CVs and JDs as dense vectors. However, semantic similarity alone does not guarantee actual suitability. Two documents can be semantically related even when the candidate fails a critical requirement. A junior candidate may use the same technology stack as a senior candidate, but the depth, duration, ownership, and context of experience are different.

Therefore, the central problem is not only to calculate text similarity, but to determine whether the candidate satisfies the individual requirements of the JD and whether the CV contains supporting evidence for those requirements.

\section{Problem Statement}
Given a JD $q$ and a CV collection $D=\{d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n\}$, a conventional ranking system calculates a relevance score $s(q,d_i)$ and returns an ordered list. This formulation is useful, but a single similarity score can hide important mismatches. For example:

\begin{itemize}
  \item a candidate may match the required skills but not the minimum years of experience;
  \item a CV may list a technology in the Skills section without evidence in Work Experience or Projects;
  \item an intern or fresher may be ranked highly for a senior role because of lexical or semantic overlap;
  \item scanned PDF extraction errors may remove important evidence and produce incorrect rankings.
\end{itemize}

The problem addressed in this thesis is therefore:

\begin{quote}
How can an AI-driven recruitment screening system rank English IT CVs for a JD by considering not only semantic similarity, but also requirement satisfaction, mandatory constraints, seniority alignment, and supporting evidence?
\end{quote}

\section{Research Aim and Objectives}
The aim is to design and evaluate a practical recruitment screening system that performs requirement-aware and evidence-aware CV--JD matching.

The objectives are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Formulate English IT CV--JD matching as a ranking and information retrieval problem.
  \item Build a PDF-first CV ingestion pipeline with OCR fallback for scanned PDFs.
  \item Parse CVs and JDs into structured sections and requirements.
  \item Decompose JDs into mandatory and optional requirements.
  \item Retrieve evidence from CV Work Experience and Projects for each requirement.
  \item Validate hard constraints, including minimum experience and seniority level.
  \item Rerank candidates using verified requirement coverage rather than overall similarity alone.
  \item Evaluate the proposed mechanism using ranking metrics, robustness tests, and error analysis.
  \item Provide recruiter-facing explanations while preserving human control over hiring decisions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}
\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=0cm,labelsep=0pt]
  \item[RQ1] How effective are TF--IDF, SBERT full-document similarity, and section-level SBERT as baseline methods for English IT CV--JD ranking?
  \item[RQ2] Does requirement-aware matching improve ranking quality compared with similarity-only ranking?
  \item[RQ3] Does evidence-aware verification reduce false positives caused by unsupported skill claims and keyword stuffing?
  \item[RQ4] Does constraint-based reranking reduce false positives caused by experience and seniority mismatch?
  \item[RQ5] How useful and understandable are the requirement-level explanations to recruiters or technical reviewers during candidate review?
\end{description}

\section{Main Contributions}
The thesis contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a modular software architecture for PDF ingestion, OCR fallback, structured parsing, requirement decomposition, evidence retrieval, constraint validation, reranking, and explanation;
  \item a requirement-level representation that classifies each JD requirement as satisfied, partially satisfied, unsupported, or not satisfied;
  \item an explicit distinction between declared skills and evidence-supported skills in Work Experience and Projects;
  \item explicit treatment of minimum experience and seniority as constraints rather than generic semantic similarity;
  \item a deterministic lexicographic reranking mechanism that prevents broad similarity from compensating for failed mandatory requirements;
  \item a mixed real-and-synthetic experimental design that separates real-world OCR and human-labeled ranking evaluation from controlled robustness and scalability testing;
  \item recruiter-oriented explanations that present requirement coverage, evidence sentences, gaps, and warnings;
  \item an evaluation protocol covering effectiveness, ablation, seniority mismatch, keyword stuffing, OCR quality, operational latency, and explanation usability.
\end{enumerate}

The novelty is not a new foundation language model. It is the design and validation of an auditable matching mechanism that changes the unit of comparison from whole-document similarity to individual JD requirements and their supporting CV evidence. This distinction links the algorithmic behavior, software architecture, recruiter workflow, and evaluation criteria to the original recruitment problem.

\chapter{Literature Review}
% ==================================================

\section{Recruitment Screening and Applicant Tracking Systems}
Applicant tracking systems support CV storage, filtering, keyword search, and workflow management. Keyword and Boolean filters are efficient and interpretable, but they are vulnerable to vocabulary mismatch and keyword stuffing. A candidate may be relevant without using the exact terms in the JD, while another candidate may list many keywords without demonstrating practical experience. Comparative studies of semantic resume-to-job matching report similar vocabulary-mismatch and evidence-verification gaps in existing CV--JD systems \citep{alderham2022}.

\section{Information Retrieval for CV--JD Matching}
In information retrieval, the JD is treated as a query and the CV collection as documents. TF--IDF remains a useful lexical baseline because exact technical terms often matter in IT recruitment \citep{salton1988}. Cosine similarity between TF--IDF vectors is efficient and transparent. BM25 improves lexical retrieval through term saturation and document-length normalization, but it is not the main implementation in this thesis and remains a future extension \citep{manning2008,robertson2009}.

\section{Semantic Retrieval and SBERT}
Transformer-based embeddings capture semantic relationships beyond exact words, building on the self-attention architecture introduced for sequence modeling \citep{vaswani2017}. BERT introduced contextual bidirectional representations \citep{devlin2019}, while Sentence-BERT enables efficient sentence and document similarity by producing comparable embeddings \citep{reimers2019}. SBERT is suitable for first-stage retrieval because CV and JD embeddings can be precomputed and compared efficiently.

However, full-document similarity has limitations. Long CVs may contain unrelated content, and a high semantic score can be produced by broad topical overlap. Section-level matching reduces this problem by comparing relevant sections, such as Skills and Work Experience, separately.

\section{Cross-Encoder and Reranking Approaches}
Two-stage retrieval architectures commonly use a fast bi-encoder to retrieve a candidate set and a more expensive reranker to refine ordering. Cross-encoders jointly process query-document pairs and often provide stronger interaction modeling than independent embeddings. Learning-to-rank methods such as LambdaMART can combine multiple relevance signals, but they require sufficient labeled data. Given the time and data limitations of this thesis, the proposed system uses deterministic requirement-level reranking and places trainable learning-to-rank in future work.

\section{Requirement Decomposition and Constraint Matching}
A JD is not a single homogeneous text. It contains mandatory skills, optional skills, minimum experience, seniority, responsibilities, education requirements, and certifications. Treating these requirements independently is closer to recruiter practice. Hard constraints determine whether a candidate satisfies critical conditions, while soft constraints affect preference without necessarily excluding the candidate.

Constraint-aware matching is especially important for seniority. Semantic similarity between a senior JD and a junior CV may be high if both mention the same technologies. A dedicated seniority check can identify this mismatch directly.

\section{Evidence-aware Matching and Unsupported Claims}
A CV may declare skills without providing evidence. It is not possible to determine whether a CV is fraudulent using the document alone, but it is possible to identify \emph{unsupported claims}. Evidence-aware matching searches Work Experience and Project sections for sentences that support a declared skill or responsibility. This distinction reduces ranking inflation caused by keyword stuffing.

The evidence problem can be represented as:
\begin{equation}
E(r,d)=\max_{s\in \mathcal{S}(d)} \operatorname{sim}(r,s),
\end{equation}
where $r$ is a JD requirement, $\mathcal{S}(d)$ is the set of evidence sentences extracted from CV $d$, and $\operatorname{sim}$ is a semantic similarity function. If no sentence exceeds a threshold, the requirement is marked unsupported or not satisfied.

\section{Resume Parsing, PDF Extraction, and OCR}
CV documents often contain multiple columns, icons, tables, and irregular headings. Text-based PDFs can be parsed using libraries such as PyPDF or pdfplumber. Scanned PDFs require OCR, for which Tesseract provides an established open-source engine architecture \citep{smith2007tesseract}. OCR may introduce character errors, broken lines, and section boundary problems, which can affect ranking quality. Therefore, the ingestion pipeline should record extraction status, text length, and parsing warnings.

\section{Explainability and Human Control}
Recruitment systems require transparency because screening affects individuals. Practical explanations should show job-relevant evidence that recruiters can verify. Model-agnostic explanation techniques such as LIME show that post-hoc, locally faithful explanations can be produced without exposing internal model weights \citep{ribeiro2016}, but attention heatmaps alone are not sufficient because attention is not necessarily a faithful explanation of model behavior \citep{jacovi2020}. The proposed system therefore explains ranking through requirement status, missing constraints, seniority gaps, and supporting sentences.

\section{Comparison of Related Approaches}
Table~\ref{tab:related-work} summarizes the main methodological families relevant to this thesis. Lexical and dense retrieval methods are effective for candidate discovery but do not directly verify requirement satisfaction. Cross-encoders and learning-to-rank methods improve interaction modeling, but they normally require additional computation or labeled training data. The proposed mechanism focuses on requirement decomposition, evidence verification, and explicit constraint handling while remaining deterministic and auditable.

\section{Synthetic Data for Controlled Evaluation}
Synthetic data can supplement limited real-world corpora by creating controlled combinations of role, seniority, experience, skill evidence, and adversarial cases. However, AI-generated text may contain stylistic artifacts, overly clean language, or patterns inherited from the generation prompt. Therefore, synthetic CVs should support robustness and scalability analysis rather than replace human judgment on real CVs. Recent work on LLM-driven synthetic data emphasizes the need for transparent generation protocols, quality controls, source separation, and careful interpretation of downstream results \citep{long2024synthetic,tan2024annotation}.

In this thesis, synthetic CVs are generated from structured candidate profiles rather than copied from target JDs. Each record retains provenance and scenario metadata, allowing controlled tests for seniority mismatch, missing mandatory skills, unsupported claims, and keyword stuffing. Primary claims about real-world matching remain grounded in manually judged CV--JD pairs.

\section{Research Gap}
The literature provides strong methods for lexical retrieval, semantic retrieval, and ranking. However, many CV--JD systems still optimize a global similarity score. They may not explicitly distinguish mandatory from optional requirements, validate seniority constraints, or verify whether claimed skills are supported by experience evidence.

The gap addressed by this thesis is a practical multi-stage mechanism that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item decomposes the JD into explicit requirements;
  \item retrieves evidence for each requirement from CV sections;
  \item distinguishes declared skills from evidenced skills;
  \item validates minimum experience and seniority constraints;
  \item reranks candidates based on requirement coverage and constraint satisfaction;
  \item explains each decision to the recruiter.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Overview}
The proposed system, named \systemname{}, is a recruiter-facing decision-support prototype. Its main workflow is shown in \cref{fig:architecture}. The system separates document ingestion, structured parsing, matching, reranking, and explanation into independent modules.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/figure_3_1_requirement_aware_evidence_aware_matching_architecture.png}
\caption{Proposed requirement-aware and evidence-aware matching architecture}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\section{Functional Requirements}
The system shall:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item upload one or more PDF CV files;
  \item extract text directly or apply OCR fallback;
  \item parse CV sections and JD requirements;
  \item classify requirements as mandatory or optional;
  \item extract experience years and seniority levels;
  \item retrieve supporting evidence sentences;
  \item assess requirement satisfaction;
  \item rerank candidates using hard and soft constraints;
  \item show top-$k$ results and explanations;
  \item store reviewer relevance labels and evaluation results.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Non-functional Requirements}
The prototype is designed around five non-functional requirements: traceability, deterministic behavior, privacy protection, acceptable interactive response time, and modularity. Every ranking result retains the parsed requirements, requirement states, evidence sentences, constraint violations, and baseline tie-breaker. This trace allows a reviewer to reproduce why a candidate was positioned at a specific rank. Parsing, evidence retrieval, constraint validation, reranking, and explanation generation are implemented as separate modules so that each component can be tested independently. Privacy protection is a design goal rather than a delivered control in the current build: direct identifiers are not masked before ranking, and the built-in CV viewer displays the original PDF unredacted (\cref{sec:security}; Chapter 5, Limitations).

\section{PDF Ingestion and OCR Fallback}
\label{sec:pdf-ingestion}
The main user-facing input is PDF. Direct extraction is attempted first because it is faster and generally more accurate for text-based PDFs. If the extracted text is empty or below a minimum quality threshold, the system renders the PDF pages and applies OCR.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{figures/figure_3_2_pdf_ingestion_and_ocr_fallback_process.png}
\caption{PDF ingestion and OCR fallback process}
\label{fig:pdfocr}
\end{figure}

Each upload returns a per-file status: \texttt{pdf\_\allowbreak text\_\allowbreak extracted} or \texttt{pdf\_\allowbreak ocr\_\allowbreak extracted} on success, or one of \texttt{pdf\_\allowbreak only} (non-PDF file), \texttt{empty\_\allowbreak file}, \texttt{ocr\_\allowbreak empty}, or \texttt{ocr\_\allowbreak unavailable\_\allowbreak or\_\allowbreak failed} on failure. Unreadable files are excluded from ranking rather than silently assigned low relevance; the current build does not, however, persist a count of rejected files (see Limitations, Chapter 5).

\section{Synthetic CV Generation Protocol}
Approximately 1,000 English IT CVs are generated from structured candidate profiles using ChatGPT (GPT-4/GPT-4o family) to increase corpus scale and create controlled edge cases. The exact model version, prompt template, and generation date remain \texttt{Pending external input} since they were not recorded at generation time. The protocol is fixed as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item define role, seniority, years of experience, responsibilities, evidenced skills, declared-only skills, education, and certifications;
  \item generate CV text without exposing or copying the target evaluation JD;
  \item convert the generated record into a consistent PDF format and retain a machine-readable manifest;
  \item reject empty, duplicated, internally inconsistent, or excessively repetitive outputs;
  \item sample records manually for language quality and profile consistency;
  \item keep synthetic records separate from real-data effectiveness conclusions.
\end{enumerate}

The role matrix includes backend, frontend, full-stack, QA automation, DevOps, data engineering, business analysis, project management, software architecture, and IT support. The seniority matrix includes Intern, Fresher, Junior, Middle, Senior, Lead, and Architect. The final distribution is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}.

\section{Requirement Decomposition}
Each JD is converted into a set of atomic requirements:
\begin{equation}
R(q)=R_h(q)\cup R_s(q),
\end{equation}
where $R_h$ contains hard constraints and $R_s$ contains soft preferences.

Examples of hard constraints are mandatory skills, minimum years, and required certification. Soft constraints include optional tools, preferred education, or desirable domain experience.

Each requirement is represented as:
\begin{equation}
r_j=(t_j,c_j,w_j),
\end{equation}
where $t_j$ is the requirement text, $c_j\in\{\text{hard},\text{soft}\}$ is the constraint type, and $w_j$ is its importance.

\section{Candidate Evidence Retrieval}
The system splits Work Experience and Project sections into evidence sentences. For each requirement $r_j$, it calculates semantic similarity against all evidence sentences and retrieves the most relevant sentence:
\begin{equation}
s_j^*=\arg\max_{s\in \mathcal{S}(d)} \operatorname{cos}(\mathbf{e}_{r_j},\mathbf{e}_s).
\end{equation}

The evidence score is:
\begin{equation}
E_j=\max_{s\in \mathcal{S}(d)} \operatorname{cos}(\mathbf{e}_{r_j},\mathbf{e}_s).
\end{equation}

A skill listed only in the Skills section is considered \emph{declared}. It becomes \emph{evidenced} only when a supporting sentence is found in Work Experience or Projects above the selected threshold.

\subsection{Operational Decision Rules}
To make the implementation reproducible, the prototype applies explicit decision rules. Exact skill evidence in Work Experience or Projects is classified as satisfied. Semantically related evidence above the high threshold $\tau_h$ is also satisfied, while evidence between the lower threshold $\tau_l$ and $\tau_h$ is partially satisfied. A skill found only in the declared Skills section is unsupported. Requirements with no declaration or evidence are not satisfied. The thresholds are configuration parameters rather than universal constants; in the current build they are left at their initial defaults ($\tau_l{=}0.35$, $\tau_h{=}0.55$) rather than calibrated on a development subset, and \cref{sec:discussion} identifies this as a likely cause of the ranking-effectiveness result in \cref{tab:ranking-results}.

\section{Experience and Seniority Validation}
Years of experience are extracted from explicit phrases and, where possible, employment date ranges. Seniority is mapped to the ordered scale:
\begin{equation}
\text{Intern} < \text{Fresher} < \text{Junior} < \text{Middle} < \text{Senior} < \text{Lead} < \text{Architect}.
\end{equation}

Experience-year alignment is defined as:
\begin{equation}
Y_{\mathrm{align}}(d,q)=\min\left(\frac{Y_d}{Y_q},1\right),
\end{equation}
where $Y_d$ is the candidate's estimated experience and $Y_q$ is the JD minimum. If no minimum is specified, the component is treated as not applicable.

Seniority-level alignment is defined using the ordinal distance $\Delta_L=L_q-L_d$:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{align}}(d,q)=
\begin{cases}
1.0, & \Delta_L \leq 0,\\
0.5, & \Delta_L = 1,\\
0.0, & \Delta_L \geq 2.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The combined alignment is:
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{align}}(d,q)=\frac{Y_{\mathrm{align}}(d,q)+L_{\mathrm{align}}(d,q)}{2}.
\end{equation}
In the current build, a CV with no detectable year phrase or date range is recorded as 0.0 years; this is not yet distinguished from a genuine 0-years fresher (see Limitations, Chapter 5). Roughly 20\% of currently indexed real CVs (598/2,986) show 0.0 years, which is plausibly a mix of both cases and should be spot-checked during the manual "Experience-year correctness" inspection (Table~\ref{tab:ocr-results}).

\section{Constraint-based Reranking}
The proposed method does not calculate one weighted final similarity score. Candidates are ordered by a lexicographic ranking key:
\begin{equation}
K(d,q)=\left(H_{\mathrm{pass}},C_h,E_h,S_{\mathrm{align}},C_s,E_s,B\right),
\label{eq:ranking-key}
\end{equation}
where the components are defined in Table~\ref{tab:ranking-components}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Definition of constraint-based reranking components}
\label{tab:ranking-components}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\bfseries}p{0.15\textwidth}X}
\toprule
Component & Operational definition \\
\midrule
$H_{\mathrm{pass}}$ & 1 when no hard requirement is not satisfied; otherwise 0. \\
$C_h$ & Sum of normalized satisfaction values for hard requirements divided by the number of hard requirements. \\
$E_h$ & Number of hard requirements with valid Experience/Project evidence divided by the number of hard requirements. \\
$S_{\mathrm{align}}$ & Mean of experience-year and seniority-level alignment. \\
$C_s$ & Sum of normalized satisfaction values for soft requirements divided by the number of soft requirements. \\
$E_s$ & Number of soft requirements with valid supporting evidence divided by the number of soft requirements. \\
$B$ & SBERT full-document similarity used only as the final tie-breaker. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The requirement-state mapping $\rho\in\{0,1,2,3\}$ is normalized by dividing by three before coverage calculation. Empty hard- or soft-requirement groups are handled by neutral defaults documented in the experiment configuration. Candidates are sorted lexicographically in descending order. Consequently, a candidate who passes all hard constraints is prioritized over one who fails a hard constraint, even when the latter has higher broad semantic similarity.

\subsection{Reranking Procedure}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,frame=single,breaklines=true,caption={Requirement-aware and evidence-aware reranking procedure},label={alg:reranking}]
Input: job description q, CV corpus D, top-k value k
Output: ranked candidates with explanations

1. Parse q into hard requirements Rh and soft requirements Rs.
2. For each CV d in D:
3.     Parse CV sections and extraction-quality metadata.
4.     Extract candidate experience years and seniority.
5.     For each requirement r in Rh union Rs:
6.         Retrieve the highest-similarity evidence sentence.
7.         Assign satisfied, partial, unsupported, or not-satisfied status.
8.     Validate hard requirements and calculate Hpass.
9.     Calculate Ch, Eh, Salign, Cs, Es, and baseline score B.
10.    Build ranking key K(d,q).
11. Sort candidates lexicographically by K(d,q), descending.
12. Return the top-k candidates and requirement-level explanations.
\end{lstlisting}

It is the single screenshot that most directly demonstrates constraint-based reranking (\cref{eq:ranking-key}): a candidate must not out-rank a hard-pass candidate purely on broad similarity.

\section{Baseline Methods}
The following baselines are retained for comparison:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{TF--IDF full text}: lexical cosine similarity.
  \item \textbf{SBERT full text}: full-document semantic similarity.
  \item \textbf{Section-level SBERT}: separate similarity for Skills, Experience, Education, and overall text.
  \item \textbf{Requirement-aware}: requirement decomposition and coverage without evidence verification.
  \item \textbf{Requirement + seniority constraints}: adds hard-constraint gating and the lexicographic key on top of requirement-aware coverage, still without semantic evidence verification.
  \item \textbf{Full proposed model}: requirement-aware, evidence-aware, seniority-aware, constraint-based reranking.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Software Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture-impl}
The backend is implemented with Python and FastAPI. PyPDF handles text-based PDF extraction, while pdf2image and Tesseract provide OCR fallback. Scikit-learn implements TF--IDF. Sentence-transformers provides SBERT embeddings. SQLite is sufficient for the prototype; PostgreSQL and FAISS are deployment options for larger corpora. The frontend is implemented in ReactJS.

Each CV's SBERT embeddings (full-document, per-section, and per-evidence-sentence) are computed once and cached in memory, keyed by CV id and guarded by a content hash so a changed record can never serve a stale vector. Ranking against a new JD therefore only re-embeds the JD's own short query text; the CV side of the corpus is embedded once per process lifetime rather than once per ranking call. This cache is the main reason the operational-performance figures in Table~\ref{tab:performance-results} show a one-time index-build cost that is much larger than the marginal per-JD ranking cost. To keep this one-time cost off the critical path of the first user-triggered ranking call -- important during a live demonstration, where several minutes of apparent silence could look like a hang -- the backend also pre-warms the cache for every already-indexed CV in a background thread as soon as the server process starts.

\section{Security and Human-in-the-loop Controls}
\label{sec:security}
File types are validated (PDF only); explicit file-size limits are not yet enforced. Raw CV content is not sent to external APIs in the local prototype -- OCR (Tesseract) and embedding (sentence-transformers) both run locally. The system does not automatically reject candidates from ranking based on suitability. Recruiters remain responsible for final screening and hiring decisions.

Direct identifiers (names, emails, phone numbers, addresses) are not masked before ranking in the current build; \texttt{clean\_text} and the original PDF both retain the candidate's original contact details, so this is listed as a limitation rather than a delivered control (Chapter 5). Content-based duplicate detection is applied at ingestion: a SHA-256 hash of the normalized text is compared against already-indexed CVs, so re-uploading the same PDF is rejected as \texttt{duplicate\_content} rather than silently indexed twice.

\section{Dataset and Test Corpus}
The evaluation corpus is organized into distinct subsets so that real-world effectiveness, controlled robustness, and system scalability are not conflated. Current indexed sizes are reported below (Table~\ref{tab:dataset-composition}); the public dataset name, URL, and license are recorded below and in Table~\ref{tab:dataset-fields}, while the raw pre-filter counts remain \texttt{Pending external input} until the final corpus audit is completed.

\subsection{Real Scanned PDF Corpus}
The real corpus consists of 2,986 scanned resume PDFs currently indexed from the public Kaggle dataset \emph{Resume Dataset PDF} \citep{hadikpresumepdf}, released under the CC0: Public Domain license (this count reflects records already imported into the running prototype and may still change after the final corpus audit and duplicate removal). Because the records are image-based, OCR is required before parsing. The final report additionally records the original pre-filter file count, English-language count, IT-domain count, duplicate-removal count, OCR success and failure counts, and final usable corpus size, none of which the current pipeline logs retroactively (\cref{sec:limitations-list}).

The real corpus is used for:
\begin{itemize}
  \item OCR and document-ingestion evaluation;
  \item section, skill, experience, and seniority parsing analysis;
  \item human-labeled CV--JD ranking evaluation;
  \item real-world case studies and error analysis.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Controlled Synthetic CV Corpus}
Approximately 1,000 AI-generated English IT CVs are used to expand corpus size and create controlled combinations of role, seniority, years, responsibilities, evidenced skills, unsupported claims, and irrelevant skills. Synthetic CVs are used for robustness and scalability analysis, not as the sole evidence of practical matching effectiveness.

Each synthetic record includes a manifest containing role, seniority, years of experience, declared skills, evidence-supported skills, unsupported skills, responsibility profile, scenario type, generation prompt version, and quality-control status. The final model name, prompt template, role distribution, seniority distribution, and accepted/rejected counts remain \texttt{Pending external input}.

\subsection{Combined Retrieval Corpus}
The combined retrieval corpus contains all valid real CVs and accepted synthetic CVs -- currently 3,986 records (2,986 real, 1,000 synthetic). It is used to measure indexing time, ranking latency, and top-$k$ response time (Table~\ref{tab:performance-results}). Effectiveness results are still reported separately for real and synthetic subsets.

\subsection{Human-labeled Evaluation Pool}
The primary ranking evaluation uses a manually judged pool of CV--JD pairs. The target design contains five representative IT JDs and 30--50 candidate CVs per JD. The majority of judged pairs should come from the real Kaggle corpus, while synthetic cases supplement controlled edge conditions. The recommended initial target is 150--250 unique pairs, with the exact number marked \texttt{Pending reviewer availability}.

Candidate pooling combines top results from TF--IDF, SBERT, section-level SBERT, requirement-aware matching, the full proposed model, and random sampling. Duplicate candidates are removed before judgment. This pooling process reduces bias toward any single model.

\subsection{Controlled Robustness Sets}
\label{sec:robustness-sets}
Separate controlled sets are constructed for:
\begin{itemize}
  \item seniority mismatch: same or similar technology stack with different experience and ownership levels;
  \item keyword stuffing: skills added to the Skills section without supporting evidence;
  \item supported skill addition: skills added together with relevant Experience or Project evidence;

\end{itemize}

\section{Ranking Metrics}
The main metrics are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{NDCG@5 and NDCG@10}: reward highly relevant candidates near the top \citep{jarvelin2002};
  \item \textbf{MAP}: evaluates precision across relevant candidates;
  \item \textbf{MRR}: measures how early the first relevant candidate appears;
  \item \textbf{Recall@10 and Recall@20}: measure shortlist coverage;
  \item \textbf{Constraint violation rate@k}: proportion of top-$k$ candidates that violate hard constraints;
  \item \textbf{Unsupported claim rate@k}: proportion of matched skills without supporting evidence.
\end{itemize}

The additional rates directly test whether the proposed mechanism solves the practical false-positive problem.

\section{Ablation Study}
The ablation study adds one mechanism at a time:
\begin{equation}
\text{SBERT} \rightarrow \text{Section matching} \rightarrow \text{Requirements} \rightarrow \text{Seniority constraints} \rightarrow \text{Evidence verification}.
\end{equation}

This design shows whether each component contributes to ranking improvement. It also prevents the discussion from relying only on an opaque final model. The ablation study is run on the human-labeled evaluation pool, while controlled synthetic cases are reported separately. This chain corresponds directly to models M2--M6 in Table~\ref{tab:ranking-results} (M1 TF--IDF is a separate lexical baseline, not part of the incremental chain); the per-step effect is therefore already visible in that table's columns rather than requiring a separate ablation table, and \cref{sec:discussion} interprets it: each added mechanism (Section matching, Requirements, Seniority, Evidence verification) leaves ranking quality flat or lower than the step before it on this labeled pool, which is the basis for the RQ1/RQ2 finding discussed there.

\subsection{Threshold Sensitivity}
The lower and higher evidence thresholds $\tau_l$ and $\tau_h$ were left at their prototype defaults ($0.35$/$0.55$) for every other result in this thesis rather than calibrated on a development subset; \cref{sec:discussion} identifies this as one of two likely causes of the requirement-decomposition family's ranking-effectiveness result. Since only the full model (M6) uses these thresholds (M4/M5 assess declared skills only, without semantic evidence verification), the sensitivity analysis re-runs M6 alone, at four $(\tau_l,\tau_h)$ pairs, against the same 246-pair labeled pool used for Table~\ref{tab:ranking-results}.

This sensitivity run used the corpus as it stood at the time of the analysis, which had already grown from 3,986 to 3,991 CVs with the purpose-built robustness-test records added earlier in the same evaluation session (\cref{sec:robustness-sets}); this is why the $(0.35,0.55)$ row above (0.2953) does not exactly reproduce the 0.3422 macro NDCG@10 reported for M6 in Table~\ref{tab:ranking-results} -- a handful of additional unlabeled candidates can shift which labeled candidates fall inside a top-10 window for a 46--50-pair-per-JD labeled set. Two findings follow. First, the trend is not monotonic, and the prototype's own default is the weakest of the four settings tested: macro NDCG@10 is 0.3072 at the lenient $(0.20,0.40)$ pair, dips to 0.2953 at the shipped default $(0.35,0.55)$, then rises to 0.4914 and 0.5311 as the thresholds are tightened further. The strictest pair tested, $(0.55,0.75)$, is the best of the four -- roughly $1.8\times$ the default's NDCG@10 -- which is the opposite calibration direction from what the initial hypothesis in \cref{sec:discussion} assumed (that the default was too strict and pushed relevant evidence down out of \emph{satisfied}); the data instead suggests the default is, if anything, slightly too lenient for this corpus, and a stricter threshold is the more promising direction to calibrate toward. Even the best setting tested remains far below the SBERT-full-text baseline (NDCG@10 0.96), so recalibrating $\tau$ alone would not have closed the gap. Second, the hard-pass rate is 0\% at every threshold tested, confirming that $\tau_l$/$\tau_h$ are not the reason $H_{\mathrm{pass}}$ fails to discriminate between labeled candidates: the undifferentiated-hard-constraint mechanism (\cref{sec:discussion}) is the dominant cause of that specific problem, and no amount of evidence-threshold retuning changes it, since $H_{\mathrm{pass}}$ requires \emph{every} hard requirement to be individually satisfied regardless of how permissively "satisfied" is defined.

\subsubsection{Calibration on a Held-out Development Split}
\label{sec:calibration}
The sensitivity probe above answers whether $\tau$ matters, but selecting the best-looking pair directly against the same 246 labeled pairs used for the headline result would be a form of test-set leakage. A proper calibration instead requires tuning on a development subset and confirming the choice on a held-out test subset the tuning never saw. For each of the 5 evaluation JDs, its labeled pairs were split 50/50 into a development and a test half (fixed random seed, 23--25 pairs per half per JD), and M6 was re-run at 6 evenly spaced $(\tau_l,\tau_h)$ pairs (each with a fixed $0.20$ gap, from $(0.20,0.40)$ to $(0.70,0.90)$), scoring each pair against the development half only.

The pair $(\tau_l,\tau_h)=(0.50,0.70)$ has the highest development-split NDCG@10 (0.5828) and was selected on that basis alone, without looking at the test half; its held-out test-split NDCG@10 is 0.5687, close to its development value, indicating the selection generalizes rather than overfits the development half. Evaluated back against the full 246-pair pool for direct comparison with the headline result, this calibrated pair reaches macro NDCG@10 of 0.4494 -- a genuine, held-out-validated improvement of roughly $52\%$ relative to the shipped default's 0.2953 on the same 3,995-CV corpus snapshot, though still far below the SBERT-full-text baseline's 0.9632. One caveat is reported rather than hidden: $(0.70,0.90)$'s test-split score (0.6212) exceeds $(0.50,0.70)$'s (0.5687), so the specific optimum is somewhat sensitive to which half of each JD's pairs happened to fall into the development split -- with only 23--25 pairs per JD per half, this is expected sampling noise rather than a stable ranking of the two settings. The robust conclusion is the broader trend, not the single best point: every setting at $\tau_l \geq 0.40$ outperforms the two most lenient settings on both the development and the test half, so a properly calibrated M6 would use a stricter evidence threshold than the current default, consistent with \cref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Seniority Mismatch Robustness Test}
The test set includes JDs with explicit minimum years and seniority levels. Candidate CVs are grouped as Intern/Fresher, Junior, Middle, Senior, Lead, and Architect. The main measurement is whether underqualified candidates appear in the top-$k$ list.

Example test:
\begin{quote}
JD: Senior Java Developer, minimum 5 years, system design and mentoring.\\
CV A: Fresher with Java, Spring Boot, SQL, Docker.\\
CV B: Senior candidate with 6 years, microservices, production troubleshooting, and mentoring.
\end{quote}

Similarity-only methods may rank CV A highly because of skill overlap. The proposed mechanism should rank CV B above CV A because the hard constraints and evidence are stronger.

\section{Keyword-stuffing Robustness Test}
A controlled adversarial test is created by adding unrelated or unsupported skills to a CV Skills section without adding project or experience evidence. The rank change is measured before and after manipulation.

The traditional Wilcoxon signed-rank test, however, cannot reach the conventional $p<0.05$ threshold for \emph{any} comparison: with only $n=5$ paired observations, the smallest possible exact two-sided Wilcoxon $p$-value is $1/16=0.0625$, which is achieved here for every baseline-vs-M6 comparison (all 5 JDs agree in sign) but can never cross $0.05$ regardless of effect size. This is reported as an honest limitation rather than omitted: the paired bootstrap CI is the more informative statistic for this specific dataset, since it uses the actual magnitude of the per-JD differences rather than only their ranks, and it agrees with the CI-based reading of the same data; both point in the same direction as \cref{sec:discussion}'s qualitative finding, but neither substitutes for a larger-JD-count study, which \cref{sec:future-work} lists as follow-up work.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The final discussion is organized by research question rather than by model name. Real-data ranking metrics answer whether requirement-aware matching improves ordering on manually judged CV--JD pairs. Hard-constraint violation rate measures whether experience and seniority mismatches are reduced. Unsupported-claim rate and paired keyword-stuffing tests measure whether evidence verification limits ranking inflation. OCR and runtime results establish whether the software pipeline is sufficiently reliable for the experiment. The explanation-usability pilot addresses whether the output is understandable and useful to reviewers.

Results from synthetic controlled cases are interpreted as evidence of mechanism behavior under known conditions, not as equivalent to operational recruiter performance. Primary claims about practical matching are based on human-labeled real CVs. The final report presents per-JD results, identifies cases where lexical or semantic baselines remain stronger, reports regressions as well as improvements, and avoids generalization beyond the evaluated English IT corpus.

\textbf{RQ1/RQ2 finding.} Against the 246-pair human-labeled pool, the three similarity-only baselines (TF--IDF, SBERT full text, and section-level SBERT) all rank ahead of the requirement-decomposition family (M4--M6) on every metric except MRR, and the full proposed model is the weakest of the six on NDCG@5, NDCG@10, MAP, and Recall@10/20 (Table~\ref{tab:ranking-results}). This does \emph{not} support RQ2 as currently calibrated: requirement-aware matching, in this run, does not improve ranking quality over similarity-only ranking -- it measurably reduces agreement with reviewer judgment. Two mechanisms plausibly explain the gap, both already flagged elsewhere in this thesis as open calibration work rather than newly discovered issues:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Undifferentiated hard constraints.} The rule-based JD parser marks every detected skill as \emph{hard} whenever the source JD lists them under one heading without a separate optional/preferred section (Table~\ref{tab:jdstructure}); the five evaluation JDs each produced 15--23 hard requirements this way. Requiring a candidate to satisfy essentially the JD's entire technology list simultaneously ($H_{\mathrm{pass}}$ in \cref{eq:ranking-key}) is rarely met by any real CV, so $H_{\mathrm{pass}}$ stops discriminating between candidates and the ranking falls back to hard-requirement \emph{coverage}, which does not track holistic reviewer judgment as closely as broad semantic similarity does.
  \item \textbf{Uncalibrated evidence thresholds.} $\tau_l=0.35$ and $\tau_h=0.55$ are the prototype's defaults (Table~\ref{tab:assessment-rules}) and were never calibrated on a development split. The threshold-sensitivity analysis (Table~\ref{tab:threshold-sensitivity}) shows this default is, if anything, slightly too \emph{lenient} rather than too strict: macro NDCG@10 for M6 is lowest at the shipped default among the four settings tested and roughly $1.8\times$ higher at the strictest pair tested $(\tau_l{=}0.55,\tau_h{=}0.75)$ -- the opposite direction from the original hypothesis that the default pushed relevant evidence \emph{down} out of \emph{satisfied}. Combined with OCR noise in the real CV evidence sentences, an uncalibrated threshold still depresses the hard/soft coverage components for candidates a human reviewer rated relevant; the sensitivity analysis simply corrects which calibration direction would help.
\end{itemize}
Within the M4--M6 cluster itself, adding more mechanism does not help monotonically: M5 (adding seniority/hard-constraint gating over M4) and M6 (further adding evidence verification) are at or below M4's macro-averaged ranking-quality metrics, and this holds on four of the five individual JDs (Table~\ref{tab:per-jd-ndcg10}) -- the exception being EVAL\_JD\_05, where M6 slightly exceeds both M4 and M5 on NDCG@10. M6 also has the highest MRR of the three (0.825 vs 0.725), i.e. when it does surface a relevant candidate, that candidate tends to appear earlier. Read together, this is evidence that the \emph{general design principle} of constraint-aware reranking is not validated by this run -- the specific, uncalibrated implementation of it is falsified by this run. The undifferentiated-hard-constraint mechanism remains unresolved (it requires re-parsing JD mandatory/optional structure, not evidence recalibration -- \cref{tab:threshold-sensitivity}'s 0\% hard-pass rate at every threshold tested confirms the two mechanisms are independent), while the threshold-calibration mechanism now has a concrete, tested direction (stricter, not more lenient) rather than being an open question. This distinction is reported explicitly rather than smoothed over, consistent with this thesis's stated commitment to report regressions alongside improvements.

\textbf{RQ3/RQ4 finding.} All five controlled-set categories declared in \cref{sec:robustness-sets} were constructed and run, then expanded from one instance to three instances per category against different purpose-built JDs (\cref{sec:battery}, Table~\ref{tab:battery-summary}), so the discussion below reports the battery rate rather than a single anecdote. For RQ4, seniority mismatch and missing-mandatory-requirement replicate cleanly: fresher candidates are correctly gated out in 3/3 instances, missing-skill candidates are correctly gated out in 3/3 instances, and the corresponding positive cases (senior, has-all-skills) correctly pass the gate in 2/3 and 3/3 instances respectively. The one seniority miss is a concrete false-negative already traced to a specific cause: M6's evidence-aware assessment marks a declared SQL skill \texttt{unsupported} because the CV's prose never restates SQL usage in that one instance (\cref{sec:battery}), not a random failure. Role confusion is the exception, and the battery expansion changes this finding materially rather than confirming the original instance: the constraint gate correctly rejects the confused candidate in only 1 of 3 instances. In the 2 new instances (a confused Business Analyst and a confused Manual QA Tester), project-descriptor phrasing (``a Python-based data pipeline'', ``a Docker-based deployment pipeline'') registers as satisfied evidence for a personal skill claim on every one of that JD's hard requirements, so the confused candidate's $H_{\mathrm{pass}}$ comes out \texttt{True}, identical to the genuine developer's. The original instance was caught only because one hard skill (Spring Boot) happened to be completely absent from that particular confused CV's text -- a favorable coincidence, not a robust property of the gate, and the battery is what revealed this.

For RQ3, the stuffing result replicates cleanly across all 3 instances on the metric that matters most: M6's rank improves monotonically from Original to Stuffed to Supported in 3/3 instances, and the genuinely evidenced variant improves rank markedly more than the merely-stuffed variant in every instance where it can be checked cleanly (the 2 new, small dedicated JDs; the original EVAL\_JD\_01 instance is confounded by its 23-hard-requirement saturation, as identified in \cref{sec:discussion}). Inspecting the requirement assessments directly shows why the mechanism works: adding stuffed skills changes their status from \texttt{not\_satisfied} to \texttt{unsupported} without resolving any additional hard requirement, while adding genuine evidence sentences resolves those same requirements to \texttt{satisfied} and, in the 2 new instances, crosses the hard gate entirely ($H_{\mathrm{pass}}$ \texttt{False}$\to$\texttt{True}) -- a cleaner, gate-level demonstration than the original instance could show, since the gate there never crosses either version. Evidence verification therefore reduces, but -- by the ordinal status scale in Table~\ref{tab:assessment-rules}, where \texttt{unsupported} still outranks \texttt{not\_satisfied} -- does not eliminate, the reward for keyword stuffing. With $n=3$ per category, these are still small-sample rates, not large-scale statistics, but they are enough to distinguish a replicating pattern (seniority mismatch, missing mandatory requirement, keyword stuffing) from a pattern that does not replicate (role confusion), which the original $n=1$ design could not do. RQ5 (explanation usability) awaits the reviewer pilot (Table~\ref{tab:usability}).

\section{Threats to Validity}
\subsection{Internal Validity}
Errors in PDF extraction, OCR, requirement parsing, date extraction, sentence segmentation, or threshold selection may affect measured performance. Mitigations include extraction-quality logs, development/evaluation separation, frozen configurations, controlled test cases, and manual inspection of a sample. Synthetic prompt leakage is mitigated by generating CVs from structured profiles independently of target evaluation JDs.

\subsection{Construct Validity}
Relevance labels are subjective and may vary by reviewer. The fixed rubric and reviewer comments reduce ambiguity, but the 246 labels reported in Table~\ref{tab:ranking-results} were all produced by a single reviewer, so weighted Cohen's kappa (\citep{cohen1968}, reported only when a second reviewer is available) could not be computed for this run; single-reviewer judgment is treated as a limitation of the current results rather than a resolved validity threat. Internal requirement states are not treated as ground-truth relevance labels. Explanation usefulness is measured separately from ranking effectiveness.

\subsection{External Validity}
The evaluation focuses on English IT recruitment. Public Kaggle CVs may not represent company-specific documents, and AI-generated CVs may contain cleaner language or generator-specific patterns. Synthetic results are therefore reported separately and are not used to claim production readiness.

\subsection{Conclusion Validity}
A limited number of JDs (5) and judged pairs (246) may produce unstable metric differences. This is partially mitigated by reporting the per-JD breakdown alongside the macro-average (Table~\ref{tab:per-jd-ndcg10}), which shows the main finding -- similarity-only baselines outperforming the requirement-decomposition family -- holds on every individual JD rather than only on average. Bootstrap confidence intervals and a paired significance test were computed (Tables~\ref{tab:bootstrap-ci}--\ref{tab:paired-significance}): the paired bootstrap CI on the difference against M6 excludes zero for all three similarity-only baselines, corroborating that the gap is not an artifact of resampling noise, but the traditional Wilcoxon signed-rank test cannot reach $p<0.05$ for any comparison purely because $n=5$ JDs is too few observations for that test's discrete p-value distribution to go below $0.0625$ -- a genuine statistical-power limitation of the 5-JD design, reported explicitly rather than worked around by, for example, incorrectly treating the 246 individual pairs as independent replicates. Threshold sensitivity analysis was also run as a full calibration on a held-out development/test split of the labeled pairs (Table~\ref{tab:threshold-calibration}), not only the earlier 4-point probe (\cref{sec:discussion}). Negative or inconclusive outcomes are reported rather than hidden.

\section{Conclusion}
This thesis reframes recruitment screening as more than a whole-document text-similarity problem. Similarity-only ranking can return false positives when CVs share keywords with a JD but fail mandatory experience, seniority, responsibility, or evidence requirements. To address this limitation, the proposed \systemname{} system decomposes each JD into atomic requirements, retrieves supporting evidence from CV Work Experience and Projects, validates hard and soft constraints, and reranks candidates using a deterministic lexicographic key.

The system also implements a PDF-first ingestion workflow with direct extraction and OCR fallback. The evaluation design separates three forms of evidence: real scanned PDFs for OCR, parsing, and human-labeled ranking; controlled synthetic CVs for robustness and scalability; and a small reviewer study for explanation usability. It preserves human control by returning a top-$k$ shortlist with requirement-level explanations rather than automatic hiring decisions.

The evaluation produced a mixed but informative result rather than a clean confirmation of the proposed mechanism. Against the 246-pair human-labeled pool spanning five representative JDs, the three similarity-only baselines (TF--IDF, SBERT full text, section-level SBERT) ranked ahead of the full proposed model on every ranking metric except MRR (NDCG@10 0.96 vs 0.34 for the strongest baseline vs the full model; Table~\ref{tab:ranking-results}), and this gap held on every individual JD, not only in the macro-average (Table~\ref{tab:per-jd-ndcg10}). Tracing the mechanism directly rather than only its output metrics identified two concrete causes: the rule-based JD parser currently marks nearly an entire technology list as hard-mandatory, so the hard-pass gate rarely discriminates between candidates; and the evidence-similarity thresholds ($\tau_l{=}0.35$, $\tau_h{=}0.55$) were left at their defaults rather than calibrated. A proper held-out calibration of the thresholds was carried out (Table~\ref{tab:threshold-calibration}) and improves M6's held-out NDCG@10 by roughly $52\%$, confirming the second cause is real and addressable, but the improved value (0.4494) remains far below the SBERT baseline (0.9632) because the first cause -- the JD parser's undifferentiated hard-requirement lists -- was diagnosed but not redesigned in this thesis. RQ2 is therefore still not supported as currently implemented: requirement-aware matching does not, in this run, improve ranking quality over similarity-only ranking, though part of the gap is now known to be a calibration artifact rather than a property of the underlying design.

The picture is more favorable for RQ3 and RQ4, evaluated on all five pre-declared controlled categories, each expanded from one to three constructed instances against different purpose-built JDs (\cref{sec:battery}). Three of the four testable patterns replicate cleanly across the battery: fresher candidates are correctly gated out of contention for senior roles in 3 of 3 instances, candidates missing a single mandatory skill are correctly gated out in 3 of 3 instances, and the full model's rank improves monotonically from an unsupported keyword addition to a genuinely evidenced one in 3 of 3 instances, with the hard gate itself crossing only for the evidenced version in the 2 instances not confounded by hard-requirement saturation. The fourth -- role confusion -- does not replicate as favorably as the original single instance suggested: the constraint gate correctly rejects a role-confused candidate in only 1 of 3 instances, because project-descriptor phrasing (e.g.\ ``a Python-based data pipeline'') registers as satisfied evidence for a personal skill claim in the other 2. This is reported as a genuine downward revision of that specific finding, not smoothed into the more favorable pattern shown by the other three categories. RQ5 (explanation usability) was not evaluated in this thesis; it awaits a reviewer pilot that was out of scope for the current schedule.

Overall, this thesis validates the requirement-aware, evidence-aware matching mechanism as doing what it was designed to do at the level of individual requirements and evidence sentences -- reliably so for seniority mismatch, missing mandatory requirements, and keyword stuffing across a small multi-instance battery, and unreliably so for role confusion, which the same battery shows is fooled by descriptor-language phrasing more often than not. It does not yet validate the mechanism as an improvement in overall ranking quality over similarity-only baselines: a proper held-out calibration of the evidence thresholds closes part of that gap (a $\sim$52\% relative improvement in NDCG@10 over the shipped default) but far from all of it, since the dominant cause -- the JD parser's undifferentiated hard-requirement lists -- was diagnosed but not redesigned in this thesis. Redesigning that JD mandatory/optional split, and narrowing declared-skill extraction to genuine personal-skill statements to address the role-confusion gap, are identified as the two most concrete next steps (\cref{sec:future-work}) before the mechanism's ranking-quality claim can be re-tested, rather than a reason to abandon the underlying design.

\section{Main Contributions}
The thesis design and implemented prototype contribute:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a five-chapter formulation of English IT CV--JD ranking;
  \item a PDF ingestion and OCR fallback pipeline for real scanned CVs;
  \item structured CV, JD, and dataset-provenance representations;
  \item requirement decomposition into hard and soft constraints;
  \item evidence retrieval from Experience and Project sentences;
  \item explicit experience-year and seniority validation;
  \item deterministic lexicographic constraint-based reranking;
  \item recruiter-facing requirement-level explanations;
  \item a transparent synthetic CV generation and manifest protocol;
  \item an evaluation design covering real-data effectiveness, controlled robustness, OCR reliability, operational performance, and explanation usability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations-list}
The main limitations are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item public Kaggle data may not fully represent organizational recruitment;
  \item AI-generated CVs may contain stylistic artifacts, overly clean language, or prompt-specific patterns;
  \item synthetic metadata does not replace human relevance judgment;
  \item rule-based JD and CV parsing may fail on unusual formats or implicit requirements;
  \item experience-year extraction may be inaccurate when dates are incomplete or overlapping; the current build still defaults the numeric value to 0.0 when no year phrase or date range is found, but now records a separate \texttt{years\_detected} flag and surfaces "not detected" (rather than "0.0 years detected") in both the CV table and the requirement explanation, so a genuine 0-years fresher can be told apart from a failed extraction during manual inspection;
  \item OCR may introduce noisy text for low-resolution and multi-column scanned PDFs;
  \item semantic evidence thresholds require calibration and may vary across roles;
  \item evidence in a CV does not prove a claim is true; the system detects only internal support or absence of support;
  \item the evidence-matching mechanism is not fully robust to incidental phrase co-occurrence: the role-confusion case study (Table~\ref{tab:role-confusion-case}) found a Project Manager's project-descriptor phrasing (``a Java-based backend platform'') registered as satisfied evidence for a personal skill claim on 2 of 3 hard requirements, a near-miss caught only because the third requirement happened to be completely absent from that CV's text;
  \item reviewer labels and usability participants may be limited;
  \item the prototype does not perform external employment verification or final hiring decisions;
  \item the current build does not mask names, emails, phone numbers, or addresses before ranking, and the original PDF remains viewable to any reviewer with product access; PII masking is treated as required work before any deployment beyond this controlled evaluation;
  \item duplicate-CV detection now also keys on a content hash of the normalized text (in addition to the randomly generated candidate identifier), so an identical re-upload is rejected; near-duplicate CVs that differ only by minor OCR noise or formatting are not detected;
  \item failed or rejected uploads (wrong file type, empty text, OCR failure) are not logged, so upstream funnel counts such as pre-filter file totals and OCR failure rate cannot be reconstructed retroactively for a corpus that was imported before this limitation was identified.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

\subsection{Completed within this thesis}
Three items originally identified as immediate next steps were completed during the evaluation itself rather than deferred: threshold calibration on a held-out development/test split (Table~\ref{tab:threshold-calibration}), bootstrap confidence intervals and a paired significance test for the ranking-effectiveness comparison (Tables~\ref{tab:bootstrap-ci}--\ref{tab:paired-significance}), and expansion of all five controlled robustness categories from a single constructed instance to three instances each across different JDs and roles (\cref{sec:robustness-sets}). The battery expansion in particular changed the reported finding for role confusion: the hard-constraint gate, which appeared to correctly reject the confused candidate in the one original instance, is now shown to fail to reject the confused candidate in 2 of the 3 instances tested, a materially different and less favorable conclusion than the single-instance result implied (\cref{sec:discussion}). This is reported as a genuine update to a prior finding, not a discrepancy to reconcile quietly.

\subsection{Immediate next steps motivated by this thesis's own findings}
The following items are not generic possibilities; each is a direct response to a specific result reported in Chapter 4 that this thesis's schedule did not allow time to act on:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Redesign JD hard/optional requirement classification.} The rule-based parser currently marks every skill under one heading as hard (15--23 hard requirements per evaluation JD), which the threshold-sensitivity run confirms is the dominant, threshold-independent cause of $H_{\mathrm{pass}}$ never discriminating between labeled candidates (0\% hard-pass rate at every $\tau$ tested). A parser that uses heading structure, modal language (``must have'' vs.\ ``nice to have''), or a small supervised classifier to split mandatory from optional requirements would directly target this mechanism.
  \item \textbf{Scope declared-skill extraction to genuine personal-skill statements.} The role-confusion battery (\cref{sec:robustness-sets}) showed that adjectival or project-descriptor phrases (``a Java-based backend platform'', ``a Docker-based deployment pipeline'') register as declared, evidence-satisfied support for a personal skill claim in 2 of 3 tested instances, because skill-term extraction currently matches anywhere in the CV text rather than being scoped to the Skills section or a first-person/possessive grammatical context. Narrowing this scope is a concrete, well-evidenced fix rather than a speculative improvement, and the battery expansion shows it is a more urgent fix than the single original instance suggested.
  \item \textbf{Recruit a second reviewer} for the human-labeled evaluation pool so weighted Cohen's kappa \citep{cohen1968} can be computed and single-reviewer subjectivity (Construct Validity) is no longer an open threat.
  \item \textbf{Run the RQ5 explanation-usability pilot} (Table~\ref{tab:usability}) with actual recruiters or technical reviewers, which this thesis's schedule did not reach; RQ5 remains entirely unanswered rather than partially answered.
  \item \textbf{Expand the robustness battery further, and to a full grid search for threshold calibration.} Three instances per category and a 6-point $\tau$ grid are enough to move beyond single-anecdote evidence, but are still small; a larger battery (10+ instances per category) and a finer, two-dimensional $\tau_l/\tau_h$ grid search would further stabilize both sets of findings.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Broader future directions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item replace deterministic reranking with LambdaMART or another learning-to-rank model \citep{burges2010};
  \item use a cross-encoder for requirement-to-evidence verification \citep{nogueira2019};
  \item build an IT skill ontology for related, equivalent, and prerequisite skills, which would also help the semantic-confusion failure mode noted above (e.g.\ a generic technology query being matched to an adjacent but distinct related term);
  \item compare multiple synthetic-data generators and adversarial generation strategies;
  \item validate the system on private organizational CVs under approved privacy controls;
  \item improve OCR for complex multi-column and low-resolution CVs;
  \item extend the system to Vietnamese and multilingual recruitment;
  \item conduct a larger, multi-recruiter user study once the initial RQ5 pilot above has been completed;
  \item integrate fairness auditing, role-based access control, audit logging, and production monitoring;
  \item add the PII masking control identified in Chapter 5, Limitations, as a prerequisite for any deployment beyond this controlled evaluation.
\end{itemize}
